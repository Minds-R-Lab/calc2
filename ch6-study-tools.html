6.1 Parametric Curves

Parametric Derivative

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} = \frac{\dot{y}}{\dot{x}}$$
The slope of a parametric curve is the ratio of the $y$-rate to the $x$-rate. Watch out: it's NOT $dx/dy$!

Horizontal Tangent

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \iff \dot{y} = 0 \text{ and } \dot{x} \neq 0$$
When the numerator is zero but denominator is not zero.

Vertical Tangent

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \text{undefined} \iff \dot{x} = 0 \text{ and } \dot{y} \neq 0$$
When the denominator is zero but numerator is not zero.

Arc Length of Parametric Curve

$$L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt$$
The speed formula: instantaneous speed is $\sqrt{(\dot{x})^2 + (\dot{y})^2}$.

Area Under Parametric Curve

$$A = \int_\alpha^\beta y(t) \cdot x'(t)\,dt$$
This is the parametric form of $\int y\,dx$. Watch the sign: if $x(t)$ is decreasing, use negatives carefully.

6.2 Polar Coordinates

Polar to Cartesian Conversion

$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta$$
Convert a polar equation $r = f(\theta)$ to Cartesian by substitution.

Cartesian to Polar Conversion

$$r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}$$
Be careful with the angle: use the quadrant of $(x, y)$ to determine the correct $\theta$.

Polar Area (Single Curve)

$$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta [f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta$$
The crucial factor: $\frac{1}{2}$! This comes from the "pizza slice" formula: $\frac{1}{2}r^2\Delta\theta$.

Polar Area Between Two Curves

$$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta [f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\,d\theta$$
Make sure $f(\theta) \ge g(\theta)$ on $[\alpha, \beta]$, and find the intersection points correctly!

Common Polar Curves

Circle (centered at origin): $r = a$ (radius $a$)
Rose: $r = a\sin(n\theta)$ or $r = a\cos(n\theta)$ ($2n$ or $n$ petals)
Cardioid: $r = a(1 + \cos\theta)$ or $r = a(1 + \sin\theta)$
Lemniscate: $r^2 = a^2\cos(2\theta)$ (figure-eight)
Limaçon: $r = a + b\cos\theta$ (various shapes)

6.3 Calculus in Polar Coordinates

Polar Arc Length

$$L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta$$
Compare to parametric: here $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, so $(\dot{x})^2 + (\dot{y})^2 = r^2 + (r')^2$.

Polar Tangent Slope

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta}$$
This comes from differentiating $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$. Use when finding tangent lines in polar form.

Horizontal Tangent in Polar

$$\frac{dy}{d\theta} = 0 \text{ and } \frac{dx}{d\theta} \neq 0$$
Set the numerator of $dy/dx$ to zero: $r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta = 0$.

Vertical Tangent in Polar

$$\frac{dx}{d\theta} = 0 \text{ and } \frac{dy}{d\theta} \neq 0$$
Set the denominator of $dy/dx$ to zero: $r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta = 0$.

Common Mistakes Gallery

Learn from the errors students make most often. Red = wrong, Green = correct.

Mistake #1: Dividing $dy/dt$ by $dx/dt$ in the wrong order
✗ Wrong

For $x = t^2$, $y = t^3$:

$dx/dt = 2t$, $dy/dt = 3t^2$

"$dy/dx = (dx/dt)/(dy/dt) = 2t/(3t^2)$"

Backwards!

✓ Correct

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} = \frac{3t^2}{2t} = \frac{3t}{2}$$

The $y$-rate goes on top, the $x$-rate on bottom.

Why? It's $\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$ (rise over run in parametric form), not the other way around. An easy mnemonic: "to go from $x$ to $y$, divide $dy$ by $dx$".
Mistake #2: Forgetting to check where a polar curve starts and ends
✗ Wrong

For $r = \sin(3\theta)$, compute area:

"$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} \sin^2(3\theta)\,d\theta$"

This counts all petals and the origin!

✓ Correct

$\sin(3\theta) = 0$ at $\theta = 0, \pi/3, 2\pi/3, \pi, \ldots$

One petal spans $0 \le \theta \le \pi/3$:

$$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\pi/3} \sin^2(3\theta)\,d\theta$$

Why? Polar curves trace different paths for different angle ranges. The curve $r = \sin(3\theta)$ has petals only where $r \ge 0$. You must find where $r = 0$ to identify the boundaries of each petal.
Mistake #3: Forgetting the $\frac{1}{2}$ in the polar area formula
✗ Wrong

For $r = 2$, a circle of radius 2:

"$A = \int_0^{2\pi} 4\,d\theta = 8\pi$"

Correct answer is $4\pi$!

✓ Correct

$$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} 4\,d\theta = 2 \cdot 2\pi = 4\pi$$

The $\frac{1}{2}$ comes from the pizza-slice area formula: $A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\Delta\theta$.

Why? The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ is fundamental to polar area. It doesn't come from nowhere—it's the area of a circular sector: $A_{\text{sector}} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ (in radians). This is as essential as the $\frac{1}{2}$ in $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$ for triangles.
Mistake #4: Mishandling the area between two polar curves
✗ Wrong

For $r_1 = 2$ and $r_2 = 1$:

"$A = \frac{1}{2}\int [2]^2 - [1]^2\,d\theta$"

Then compute $\int (4 - 1)\,d\theta = 3\theta$ with wrong limits

Confused about what $[f(\theta)]^2$ means!

✓ Correct

$$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} [2]^2 - [1]^2\,d\theta$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} (4 - 1)\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 3 \cdot 2\pi = 3\pi$$

Why? The notation $[f(\theta)]^2$ means "the square of the function value," i.e., $(f(\theta))^2$. You plug in $\theta$ first, get a number, then square it. Also, always identify the correct integration limits from the geometry of the curves.
Mistake #5: Not recognizing that some polar curves trace over themselves
✗ Wrong

For $r = 1 - 2\cos\theta$, integrate from $0$ to $2\pi$:

"All of that region counts for the area."

But when $r < 0$, the curve backtracks!

✓ Correct

Find where $r = 0$: $\cos\theta = 1/2 \Rightarrow \theta = \pm\pi/3$

For $\pi/3 < \theta < 5\pi/3$, $r < 0$ (curve retraces).

Count the inner loop separately, or use limits $0 \le \theta \le \pi/3$ for one lobe.

Why? In polar coordinates, negative $r$ means the point is in the opposite direction from angle $\theta$. This causes curves like limaçons to loop back and retrace themselves. Always sketch or check sign changes.

6.1 Parametric Curves

Formal Definition: A parametric curve is defined by two functions: $x = f(t)$ and $y = g(t)$, where $t$ ranges over some interval. The curve traces out points $(f(t), g(t))$ as $t$ varies.
🎬 The simple version: Imagine you're recording a movie of a dot moving. At each time $t$, the dot is at position $(x(t), y(t))$. When you play the movie in fast-forward, the dot traces a path on the plane—that's your parametric curve.
Analogy: A car driving on a road. At time $t$ seconds, the car is at position $(x(t), y(t))$. The path the car took is the parametric curve. The speed of the car is $\sqrt{(\dot{x})^2 + (\dot{y})^2}$.

6.2 Polar Coordinates

Formal Definition: A point in the plane is described by $(r, \theta)$ where $r$ is the distance from the origin and $\theta$ is the angle from the positive $x$-axis. Conversion: $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$.
🎯 The simple version: Instead of saying "go right 3 units and up 4 units" (Cartesian), you say "aim at angle $\theta$ and go 5 units out" (polar). Some curves are way easier to describe in polar (like circles centered at the origin or spiral shapes).
Analogy: Giving directions. Cartesian: "Go 3 blocks east, 4 blocks north." Polar: "Head northeast at a 53° angle and walk 5 blocks." Both get you to the same place!

6.3 Calculus with Parametric & Polar

Formal Definition: For parametric curves, slopes, arc lengths, and areas are computed by converting to the chain rule and integration. For polar curves, area uses the formula $A = \frac{1}{2}\int r^2\,d\theta$.
⚙️ The simple version: It's about relating rates and areas to the parameter. For parametric: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\text{vertical speed}}{\text{horizontal speed}}$. For polar: area of a pizza slice with radius $r$ and angle $\Delta\theta$ is $\frac{1}{2}r^2\Delta\theta$—just add up all the slices!
Analogy (Parametric): You're walking along a path. Your northward speed is $dy/dt$, your eastward speed is $dx/dt$. Your slope (how steep the path is) is: "how fast I go north" divided by "how fast I go east."

Analogy (Polar Area): You have a pizza centered at the origin. Each infinitesimal slice has area $\frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta$. To find the total area of the pizza (or a chunk of it), integrate the slice areas.
