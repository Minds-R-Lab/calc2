Chapter 2.4: Arc Length and Surface Area

Introduction

Learning Objectives
  • Understand the arc length formula and its geometric interpretation
  • Calculate arc length for curves given as functions
  • Understand surface area of revolution conceptually
  • Apply the surface area formula for curves revolved around axes
  • Handle both vertical and horizontal axes of revolution

Beyond finding areas and volumes, we can use integration to find the length of a curve (arc length) and the area of a surface of revolution (surface area).

Arc length represents the "distance along" a curve, while surface area is the area of the surface you create when a curve is revolved around an axis. Both concepts have elegant formulas derived from the fundamental integral.

Theory

Definition: Arc Length Formula

The length of the curve $y = f(x)$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$ is:

$$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$$

Derivation: A small arc segment has length $ds = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2} \, dx$.

Definition: Surface Area of Revolution (Around x-axis)

When the curve $y = f(x)$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$ (with $f(x) \geq 0$) is revolved around the x-axis, the surface area is:

$$S = 2\pi \int_a^b f(x) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$$

Interpretation: The circumference of the circle at height $f(x)$ is $2\pi f(x)$, and we multiply by the arc length element $ds$.

Definition: Surface Area of Revolution (Around y-axis)

For a curve revolved around the y-axis:

$$S = 2\pi \int_a^b x \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$$

where $x$ is the distance from the y-axis.

💡 Pro Tip

The surface area formula is $2\pi \cdot (\text{distance from axis}) \cdot ds$. This is the circumference times the arc length element, which makes intuitive sense when you "unwrap" the surface.

Method: Computing Arc Length and Surface Area

Steps for Arc Length
  1. Find the derivative $f'(x)$
  2. Compute $[f'(x)]^2$
  3. Form the integrand: $\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}$
  4. Set up and evaluate $L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$
⚠️ Common Mistake

Arc length integrals are often difficult to evaluate and may require numerical methods or special techniques. Don't expect all arc length integrals to have nice closed forms. Sometimes they simplify to elementary functions by recognizing patterns like $1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$ or $1 + \sinh^2 x = \cosh^2 x$.

Steps for Surface Area
  1. Find $f'(x)$ and compute the arc length element $\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}$
  2. Determine the radius of revolution (distance from axis to curve)
  3. Set up the integral: $S = 2\pi \int_a^b (\text{radius}) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$
  4. Evaluate

Examples

Example 1: Arc Length of $y = x^{3/2}$ From 0 to 4

Find the arc length of the curve $y = x^{3/2}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$.

Solution ▼

Step 1: Find the derivative

$$f'(x) = \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}$$

Step 2: Compute the integrand

$$[f'(x)]^2 = \frac{9}{4}x$$ $$1 + [f'(x)]^2 = 1 + \frac{9}{4}x = \frac{4 + 9x}{4}$$ $$\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} = \frac{\sqrt{4 + 9x}}{2}$$

Step 3: Set up and evaluate the integral

$$L = \int_0^4 \frac{\sqrt{4 + 9x}}{2} \, dx$$

Let $u = 4 + 9x$, so $du = 9 \, dx$ and $dx = \frac{du}{9}$.
When $x = 0$, $u = 4$; when $x = 4$, $u = 40$.

$$L = \int_4^{40} \frac{\sqrt{u}}{2} \cdot \frac{du}{9} = \frac{1}{18} \int_4^{40} u^{1/2} \, du$$ $$= \frac{1}{18} \left[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_4^{40} = \frac{1}{27}[u^{3/2}]_4^{40}$$ $$= \frac{1}{27}(40^{3/2} - 8) = \frac{1}{27}(40\sqrt{40} - 8)$$ $$= \frac{1}{27}(80\sqrt{10} - 8) = \frac{8(10\sqrt{10} - 1)}{27}$$

Example 2: Arc Length of $y = \ln(\sec x)$ From 0 to $\pi/4$

Find the arc length of $y = \ln(\sec x)$ from $x = 0$ to $x = \pi/4$.

Solution ▼

Step 1: Find the derivative

$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sec x} \cdot \sec x \tan x = \tan x$$

Step 2: Compute the integrand

$$[f'(x)]^2 = \tan^2 x$$ $$1 + [f'(x)]^2 = 1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$$ $$\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} = |\sec x| = \sec x \quad \text{(for } x \in [0, \pi/4]\text{)}$$

Step 3: Set up and evaluate

$$L = \int_0^{\pi/4} \sec x \, dx = [\ln|\sec x + \tan x|]_0^{\pi/4}$$ $$= \ln(\sqrt{2} + 1) - \ln(1 + 0) = \ln(\sqrt{2} + 1)$$

Example 3: Surface Area of $y = \sqrt{x}$ From 0 to 1, Revolved About x-axis

Find the surface area when $y = \sqrt{x}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$ is revolved about the x-axis.

Solution ▼

Step 1: Find the derivative

$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$$

Step 2: Compute the arc length element

$$[f'(x)]^2 = \frac{1}{4x}$$ $$1 + [f'(x)]^2 = 1 + \frac{1}{4x} = \frac{4x + 1}{4x}$$ $$\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} = \frac{\sqrt{4x + 1}}{2\sqrt{x}}$$

Step 3: Set up and evaluate the surface area integral

$$S = 2\pi \int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt{x}} \, dx = \pi \int_0^1 \sqrt{4x+1} \, dx$$

Let $u = 4x + 1$, so $du = 4 \, dx$ and $dx = \frac{du}{4}$.
When $x = 0$, $u = 1$; when $x = 1$, $u = 5$.

$$S = \pi \int_1^5 \sqrt{u} \cdot \frac{du}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4} \left[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_1^5$$ $$= \frac{\pi}{6}[u^{3/2}]_1^5 = \frac{\pi}{6}(5\sqrt{5} - 1)$$

Practice Problems

Practice Problem 1

Find the arc length of the curve $y = \frac{1}{3}(x^2 + 2)^{3/2}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$.

Show Solution ▼

First, find the derivative:

$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{2}(x^2 + 2)^{1/2} \cdot 2x = x\sqrt{x^2 + 2}$$

Then:

$$[f'(x)]^2 = x^2(x^2 + 2) = x^4 + 2x^2$$ $$1 + [f'(x)]^2 = 1 + x^4 + 2x^2 = (x^2 + 1)^2$$ $$\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} = x^2 + 1$$

Therefore:

$$L = \int_0^1 (x^2 + 1) \, dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3} + x\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3} + 1 = \frac{4}{3}$$

Practice Problem 2

Find the surface area when $y = 3x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$ is revolved about the x-axis.

Show Solution ▼

We have $f(x) = 3x$ and $f'(x) = 3$.

$$\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$$

Therefore:

$$S = 2\pi \int_0^1 3x \cdot \sqrt{10} \, dx = 6\pi\sqrt{10} \int_0^1 x \, dx$$ $$= 6\pi\sqrt{10} \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = 3\pi\sqrt{10}$$

Practice Problem 3

Find the arc length of the curve $y = \cosh(x)$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$.

Show Solution ▼

We have $f(x) = \cosh(x)$ and $f'(x) = \sinh(x)$.

$$\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} = \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2(x)} = \sqrt{\cosh^2(x)} = \cosh(x)$$

Therefore:

$$L = \int_0^1 \cosh(x) \, dx = [\sinh(x)]_0^1 = \sinh(1) - 0 = \sinh(1)$$

Note: $\sinh(1) = \frac{e - e^{-1}}{2} \approx 1.175$
