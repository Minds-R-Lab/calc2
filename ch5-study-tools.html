5.1 Power Series

Power Series (General Form)

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n$$
Center: $a$. Coefficients: $c_n$. Variable: $x$. The series is a function of $x$.

Radius of Convergence (Ratio Test)

$$L = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left|\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\right|, \quad R = \frac{1}{L}$$
The series converges for $|x - a| < R$ and diverges for $|x - a| > R$. Always check endpoints separately!

Interval of Convergence

$$|x - a| < R \quad \text{or} \quad (a - R, a + R)$$
May include one or both endpoints after checking. Need $x$ values, not just the radius $R$.

Geometric Series (Special Case)

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} r^n = \frac{1}{1-r}, \quad |r| < 1$$
Converges when $|r| < 1$, diverges when $|r| \ge 1$. Use this to derive many Maclaurin series.

5.2 Taylor & Maclaurin Series

Taylor Series (General)

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$$
Centered at $a$. Requires all derivatives at $a$. Converges in some interval around $a$.

Maclaurin Series (Special Case)

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}x^n$$
Centered at $a = 0$. The most common Taylor series — memorize key Maclaurin expansions!

$e^x$ (Must Memorize!)

$$e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}, \quad \text{all } x$$
Converges for all real $x$. Use substitution to get $e^{-x}$, $e^{x^2}$, etc.

$\sin x$ (Must Memorize!)

$$\sin x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}, \quad \text{all } x$$
Only odd powers. Alternates in sign. Converges everywhere.

$\cos x$ (Must Memorize!)

$$\cos x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}, \quad \text{all } x$$
Only even powers. Starts at 1. Alternates in sign. Converges everywhere.

$\frac{1}{1-x}$ (Must Memorize!)

$$\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n, \quad |x| < 1$$
This is the geometric series. Use substitution for variations like $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$, $\frac{1}{2-x}$.

$\ln(1+x)$ (Must Memorize!)

$$\ln(1+x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}x^n, \quad -1 < x \le 1$$
Starts at $n=1$, not $n=0$. Converges on $(-1, 1]$, including the right endpoint.

$(1+x)^k$ (Binomial Series)

$$(1+x)^k = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \binom{k}{n} x^n, \quad |x| < 1$$
$\binom{k}{n} = \frac{k(k-1)\cdots(k-n+1)}{n!}$. Works for any real $k$, not just integers.

5.3 Taylor Remainder & Error Bounds

Taylor Remainder (Lagrange Form)

$$R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$$
$c$ is some point between $a$ and $x$. We don't know $c$, but we can bound the remainder.

Error Bound (if $|f^{(n+1)}(c)| \le M$)

$$|R_n(x)| \le \frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$$
Find an upper bound $M$ for the $(n+1)$th derivative. Use this to guarantee approximation accuracy.

Approximation Quality

$$f(x) \approx \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n, \quad |R_N(x)| < \text{error tolerance}$$
Use the error bound to find how many terms $N$ you need for a desired accuracy.

5.4 Operations on Power Series

Term-by-Term Differentiation

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} nc_n(x-a)^{n-1}$$
Can differentiate inside the series (within the interval of convergence).

Term-by-Term Integration

$$\int \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n\,dx = C + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n\frac{(x-a)^{n+1}}{n+1}$$
Can integrate inside the series. Adds a constant $C$.

Multiplication/Substitution of Series

$$\text{If } f(x) = \sum c_n x^n, \text{ then } f(u(x)) = \sum c_n [u(x)]^n$$
Substitute into a known series to get new series (e.g., $e^{-x^2}$ from $e^x$).

Common Mistakes Gallery

Learn from the errors students make most often. Red = wrong, Green = correct.

Mistake #1: Forgetting to check endpoints of the interval of convergence
✗ Wrong

$\sum \frac{(-1)^n x^n}{n}$: Ratio Test gives $R = 1$.

"So the interval is $(-1, 1)$."

Didn't check if the series converges at $x = \pm 1$!

✓ Correct

Ratio Test gives $R = 1$, so we have convergence for $|x| < 1$.

At $x = 1$: $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$ converges (Alternating Series Test).

At $x = -1$: $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges (harmonic series).

Interval: $(-1, 1]$

Why? The Ratio Test is inconclusive at the endpoints. You must plug in $x = a + R$ and $x = a - R$ separately and use other convergence tests. The interval can be open, half-open, or closed depending on what you find.
Mistake #2: Confusing radius of convergence with interval of convergence
✗ Wrong

$\sum c_n(x-2)^n$ with $R = 3$:

"The interval of convergence is $3$."

$R$ is a number, not an interval!

✓ Correct

Radius of convergence: $R = 3$ (just a number).

Interval of convergence: $|x - 2| < 3$, i.e., $-1 < x < 5$ (plus check endpoints).

Why? The radius $R$ is a single positive number. The interval is the set of $x$ values: $(a - R, a + R)$ and possibly including endpoints. Always state the interval as an interval, not just a number.
Mistake #3: Wrong factorial in the Taylor/Maclaurin formula
✗ Wrong

Maclaurin series: $f(x) = \sum \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n} x^n$

Should be $n!$ in the denominator, not $n$!

✓ Correct

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} x^n$$

The denominator is $n!$ (factorial), not $n$.

Why? The factorial $n!$ comes from the formula for the Taylor coefficients. It's not optional — forgetting it will give you wrong series. For example, $e^x = \sum x^n/n!$, not $\sum x^n/n$.
Mistake #4: Computing derivatives instead of using substitution
✗ Wrong

Find Maclaurin series for $e^{-x^2}$:

"Take the derivative... then the second derivative... then..."

This is tedious and error-prone!

✓ Correct

Use the known Maclaurin series for $e^u$:

$$e^u = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{u^n}{n!}$$

Substitute $u = -x^2$:

$$e^{-x^2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-x^2)^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{n!}$$

Why? Substituting into a known series is much faster and less error-prone than computing derivatives. Always ask: "Do I know a related series I can substitute into?" Examples: $e^{2x}$, $\sin(x^3)$, $\cos(\pi x)$, $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$.
Mistake #5: Applying the Ratio Test incorrectly to power series
✗ Wrong

$\sum \frac{x^n}{2^n}$: "Apply Ratio Test to the series directly: $L = |x|$."

Should apply Ratio Test to coefficients, not the series itself!

✓ Correct

$a_n = \frac{1}{2^n}$

$$\lim \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \lim \frac{1/2^{n+1}}{1/2^n} = \frac{1}{2}$$

So $R = 2$, and convergence when $|x| < 2$.

Why? For power series, the Ratio Test should be applied to the coefficients $c_n$ (or $a_n$ in the form $\sum a_n$), not to the full terms with $x$ included. You're finding the radius $R = 1/L$, then checking when $|x - a| < R$.
Mistake #6: Not verifying that the series actually converges to $f(x)$
✗ Wrong

"I found the Taylor series for $f(x)$, so it must equal $f(x)$ everywhere in the interval."

A Taylor series can converge without equaling the original function!

✓ Correct

A Taylor series $\sum \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$ converges in some interval, but may NOT equal $f(x)$.

The error bound $|R_n(x)| \to 0$ confirms that the series equals $f(x)$.

For most "nice" functions (polynomials, trig, exponentials, logs), the series does converge to $f(x)$ in its interval of convergence.

Why? In practice, for functions you'll encounter, if the Taylor series converges, it converges to $f(x)$. But technically, you should verify using the Lagrange remainder formula: show $|R_n(x)| \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

Explain Like I'm 5

Simple, intuitive explanations next to the formal definitions. Click the purple button to toggle.

5.1 Power Series

Formal Definition: A power series centered at $a$ is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n$. It converges for $x$ in an interval $(a-R, a+R)$ where $R$ is the radius of convergence.
🎯 The simple version: A power series is like a polynomial with infinitely many terms. Instead of $a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_n x^n$ (finite), you have infinitely many terms. But it only "works" (converges) for certain values of $x$ — those inside a "zone" of width $2R$ centered at $a$.
Analogy: Imagine a radio signal centered at a location. It broadcasts clearly within a certain radius $R$. Outside that radius, the signal fades to nothing. The power series is the same — it converges nicely for $|x-a| < R$ and diverges outside.

5.2 Taylor & Maclaurin Series

Formal Definition: The Taylor series for $f(x)$ at $x = a$ is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$. When $a = 0$, it's called a Maclaurin series.
📐 The simple version: A Taylor series is a way to write any smooth function as an infinite polynomial. The coefficients come from the derivatives of the function at one point ($a$). It's like saying: "If I know everything about a function at one spot, I can build an infinite polynomial approximation around that spot."
Analogy: Imagine you're zoomed in very close to a curve on a graph. The closer you zoom in, the more the curve looks like a straight line (the tangent line). Zoom in more, and you can see it curves — so add a quadratic term. Keep zooming and adding terms, and you can describe the entire curve as a polynomial near that point. That's the Taylor series.

5.3 Key Maclaurin Series (Must Memorize!)

Formal Definition: Common functions have well-known Maclaurin series: $e^x = \sum x^n/n!$, $\sin x = \sum (-1)^n x^{2n+1}/(2n+1)!$, $\cos x = \sum (-1)^n x^{2n}/(2n)!$, $\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum x^n$.
💾 The simple version: Rather than computing Taylor series from scratch every time using derivatives, mathematicians have already done the work for common functions. Memorize these four series, and you can use substitution to get dozens more. It's like learning multiplication tables instead of counting on your fingers every time.
Analogy: Instead of deriving $e^{-x^2}$ from scratch (tedious!), you use the fact that $e^u = 1 + u + u^2/2! + \cdots$, then plug in $u = -x^2$. Much faster!

5.4 Radius vs Interval of Convergence

Formal Definition: The radius of convergence $R$ is a number such that the series converges for $|x-a| < R$ and diverges for $|x-a| > R$. The interval of convergence is the set of all $x$ values where the series converges (including endpoints if they converge).
📏 The simple version: The radius is just a number: "The series converges within distance $R$ from the center." The interval is the actual set of $x$ values: "It works for $x$ between here and here" (including possible endpoints). Don't confuse them!
Analogy: Radius = "I can see 10 meters away" (just a distance). Interval = "I can see from the tree 10 meters to my left to the fence 10 meters to my right, but not into the locked barn at -10" (actual places, including boundary conditions).

5.5 Taylor Remainder & Approximation

Formal Definition: The error $R_n(x)$ between $f(x)$ and its $n$th-degree Taylor polynomial satisfies $|R_n(x)| \le \frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$ where $M$ bounds the $(n+1)$th derivative.
📊 The simple version: When you use only the first few terms of a Taylor series to approximate a function, you're off by some error. The formula tells you: "If you use $n$ terms, your error is at most this small." Use more terms (larger $n$) and the error shrinks rapidly (because of the factorial!).
Analogy: Imagine approximating a curve with a line (1 term), then a parabola (2 terms), then a cubic (3 terms). Each time you add a term, your approximation gets better. The formula guarantees how good each approximation is.
